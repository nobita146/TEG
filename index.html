A Study in Heat & Charge

Thermoelectricity, and the quiet border where heat becomes current.

A direct conversion between thermal energy and electrical energy — no moving parts, no fluids, no combustion. Three effects discovered across the nineteenth century still power spacecraft crossing the heliopause today. This is the physics, the materials, and the machines that turn a temperature difference into useful work.

§ 01 — Definition
The Core Idea

A temperature difference, made electrical.

Thermoelectricity is the direct, reversible conversion between a temperature gradient and an electric voltage inside a solid material. No turbines spin. No steam expands. The electrons themselves carry the heat.

Fig. 1.1 A commercial thermoelectric module, ~4 cm on a side. Inside are hundreds of bismuth-telluride couples sandwiched between ceramic plates.
Plain definition

Place one end of a metal rod in a flame and the other in ice water. Electrons at the hot end gain energy and diffuse toward the cold end, leaving the hot side slightly electron-poor and the cold side slightly electron-rich. A voltage appears across the rod — small, but real and measurable. This is the thermoelectric effect in its purest form.

The reverse also works. Push an external current through the rod and heat is carried from one end to the other: one side gets cold, the other gets hot. Heat and charge move together, joined at the scale of the electron.

Fig. 1.2 A conductor under a temperature gradient. Charge carriers diffuse from hot to cold, building a measurable voltage.
Why it matters

Roughly 60% of all energy humanity generates is lost as waste heat — from car exhausts, factory furnaces, server farms, even the back of your refrigerator. Thermoelectric converters offer a way to claw some of that back as electricity, silently and without moving parts. The same physics, run in reverse, gives us compact solid-state refrigerators and the long-lived power sources on deep-space probes.

§ 02 — The Three Effects
Seebeck · Peltier · Thomson

Three names for one phenomenon, discovered across thirty years.

Between 1821 and 1851, three European physicists — working independently and with different apparatus — described three facets of the same underlying physics. Together they form the complete classical theory of thermoelectricity.

Effect 01 / Seebeck · 1821

Heat makes a voltage.

Thomas Johann Seebeck, an Estonian-German physicist living in Berlin, noticed that a compass needle moved when he placed it near a closed loop of two different metals whose junctions were at different temperatures. He had, in fact, generated an electric current from heat — though he himself believed he was observing a magnetic effect.

The voltage produced is directly proportional to the temperature difference, and the constant of proportionality — the Seebeck coefficient, S — depends on the materials at the junction.

Fig. 2.1 The Seebeck circuit. Two dissimilar metals form a loop; one junction is heated, the other cooled. A voltage appears measurable across the loop.
V = S · ΔT
V  —  voltage produced (volts)
S  —  Seebeck coefficient of the material pair (μV / K)
ΔT  —  temperature difference between junctions (K)
Everyday use

The thermocouple — a cheap, reliable temperature sensor found in industrial furnaces, kilns, gas boilers, and jet engines — is a direct application of the Seebeck effect. Two dissimilar metal wires welded at one tip generate a voltage that varies with temperature. No battery, no electronics at the hot end, just two wires. The same physics survives decades of 1200 °C service.

Effect 02 / Peltier · 1834

Current makes cold.

Thirteen years after Seebeck, the French watchmaker-turned-physicist Jean Charles Athanase Peltier ran the experiment in reverse. He passed an electric current through a junction of two different metals and observed that one junction grew warm and the other grew cold — beyond the simple resistive heating he expected.

Heat was being absorbed or released at the junction itself, depending on the direction of current flow. Reverse the current, and hot becomes cold. This is the basis of every solid-state refrigerator and CPU cooler in use today.

Fig. 2.2 The Peltier circuit. An external current driven through a junction of two dissimilar conductors causes heat to be absorbed at one junction and released at the other.
Q̇ = Π · I
Q̇  —  rate of heat absorbed or released at the junction (watts)
Π  —  Peltier coefficient of the material pair (volts)
I  —  current through the junction (amperes)
In your computer

The small flat plates screwed onto high-end CPU and GPU coolers, the portable car-seat coolers that plug into a cigarette lighter, the wine-chilling buckets in hotel rooms — all run on the Peltier effect. Reverse the polarity of the wires and a Peltier cooler becomes a heater. The same device cools in summer and warms in winter.

Effect 03 / Thomson · 1851

A gradient, within one conductor.

The Scottish physicist William Thomson — later Lord Kelvin — realized that Seebeck and Peltier were not independent. He predicted, and then demonstrated, that a current flowing through a single homogeneous conductor with a temperature gradient along its length would also absorb or release heat along that length.

The Thomson effect is the smallest of the three, and is usually a correction term rather than a headline phenomenon. But it completed the classical picture, and Thomson's deeper insight — that the Seebeck and Peltier coefficients are related through the absolute temperature — gave us the Thomson relations, still the foundation of all thermoelectric theory.

Fig. 2.3 William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin (1824–1907). His 1851 paper connected Seebeck and Peltier through what are now called the Kelvin relations.
Π = S · T
Π  —  Peltier coefficient
S  —  Seebeck coefficient
T  —  absolute temperature (kelvin)

The first Thomson relation: the two effects are aspects of the same underlying physics, linked by temperature.
Why it matters

The Thomson relations are not just a curiosity. They tell us that any material with a non-zero Seebeck coefficient must also exhibit the Peltier effect — and vice versa. A good thermoelectric generator is, by the same token, a good thermoelectric cooler. The two devices are the same machine run in opposite directions.

"Heat and electricity are convertible into each other; the unit of heat is a definite and invariable quantity, and the equivalent of heat for one unit of electricity is also a definite and invariable quantity."

James Prescott Joule · 1843

§ 03 — Thermoelectric Materials
The Figure of Merit

The hardest problem in the field: good conductors of electricity, bad conductors of heat.

A good thermoelectric material must satisfy three contradictory demands at once. It must conduct electricity well, conduct heat poorly, and have a large Seebeck coefficient. The three are deeply linked — improving one usually degrades the others.

ZT = (S² · σ · T) / κ
ZT  —  dimensionless figure of merit (the higher, the better)
S  —  Seebeck coefficient (V / K)
σ  —  electrical conductivity (S / m)
T  —  absolute temperature (K)
κ  —  thermal conductivity (W / m·K)

A ZT of 1 is workable; ZT of 2 is excellent; ZT of 3 has been the field's grail for fifty years.
The central paradox

Why ZT is so hard to raise.

In a normal metal, the same electrons that carry charge also carry heat. So any material that conducts electricity well — high σ — also conducts heat well — high κ. The ratio S²σ/κ that defines ZT ends up fighting itself.

The breakthrough came when chemists turned to heavily doped semiconductors, where electrons carry charge but lattice vibrations (phonons) carry most of the heat. By engineering the crystal structure to scatter phonons while letting electrons pass, the two conductivities can be unlinked.

Modern thermoelectric research is largely the search for crystal structures, nanostructures, and alloy compositions that break the natural coupling between σ and κ.

Fig. 3.1 A boule of bismuth telluride, the workhorse thermoelectric material for near-room-temperature applications.
Fig. 3.2 Skutterudite crystal structure, a candidate for mid-temperature thermoelectric generation. The "rattling" atoms inside the cage scatter phonons and lower thermal conductivity.

The four families of working materials

Each family dominates a different temperature range. No single material performs well across the full span — engineers pick the material whose peak ZT falls inside the temperature range of the application.

Bi₂Te₃
Bismuth telluride
PEAK · 50–150 °C  |  ZT ≈ 1.0
The workhorse. Used in CPU coolers, picnic boxes, and small refrigerators. Alloyed with antimony and selenium for tuning.
PbTe
Lead telluride
PEAK · 300–600 °C  |  ZT ≈ 0.8–1.2
The classic mid-temperature material. Powers radioisotope thermoelectric generators in deep-space probes. Toxic; restricts civilian use.
SiGe
Silicon-germanium alloy
PEAK · 600–1000 °C  |  ZT ≈ 0.5–0.9
Survives extreme heat. Used in the multi-hundred-watt RTGs that powered Voyager, Galileo, and Cassini past Jupiter.
Half-Heusler
Half-Heusler alloys
PEAK · 400–700 °C  |  ZT ≈ 0.8–1.0
Newer, less toxic, mechanically robust. Leading candidate for industrial waste-heat recovery in steel and glass plants.
§ 04 — Generators & Coolers
Building Real Devices

From a single junction to a module.

A single thermocouple produces millivolts. To get useful voltage and current, hundreds of couples are wired electrically in series and thermally in parallel, sandwiched between two ceramic plates. The result is the thermoelectric module.

TEG — Thermoelectric Generator

Heat in. Electricity out.

A TEG is built from pairs of n-type and p-type semiconductor legs. The hot side is held against a heat source; the cold side against a heat sink. Electrons in the n-type leg and holes in the p-type leg both migrate from hot to cold — but because they carry opposite charges, the currents add rather than cancel.

The two legs are joined at the hot end by a metal interconnect, and at the cold end they connect to the external circuit. Many such pairs — typically 127 or 254 — are wired in series inside a single module to raise the output voltage to a useful range.

Efficiencies are modest: 5–8% for commercial modules, up to ~12% for the best laboratory devices. For comparison, a steam turbine reaches 35–45%. But TEGs have no moving parts, no maintenance, and work for decades.

Fig. 4.1 A single thermocouple inside a TEG module. Hundreds of these are wired in series to produce useful voltage. n-type and p-type legs carry opposite carriers, so their currents add.
Carnot bound

No heat engine can exceed the Carnot efficiency η = (Th − Tc) / Th. For a TEG running at 500 °C hot and 50 °C cold, that limit is about 60%. Real TEGs reach a fraction of Carnot, typically 10–20% of the theoretical maximum.

TEC — Thermoelectric Cooler

Electricity in. Cold out.

Run the same module in reverse. Apply a DC voltage across the cold-side terminals, and electrons stream through the n-leg from cold to hot (carrying heat with them), while holes do the same in the p-leg. The hot-side interconnect absorbs heat; the cold side releases it.

The result is a solid-state refrigerator: no compressor, no refrigerant, no noise, no vibration. Reverse the polarity and it becomes a heater. Stack them in stages and you can reach temperatures as low as −100 °C from a single room-temperature starting point.

Coefficient of performance (COP) is typically 0.4–0.7 for single-stage TECs — far below a vapor-compression fridge at COP 2–4. The trade-off is simplicity and reliability: a TEC will run for 100,000+ hours with no maintenance.

Fig. 4.2 A Peltier module with attached heatsink, used for CPU cooling in high-performance computers. The cold face sits against the processor; the hot face vents to the heatsink.
The same machine

By the Kelvin relation (see §02), a material with a high Seebeck coefficient also has a high Peltier coefficient. So the same bismuth-telluride module that generates electricity from a wood-stove's heat can be unplugged, wired to a battery, and used to chill a drink. The generator and the cooler are the same physical object, run in opposite directions.

§ 05 — Applications
Where It Actually Lives

From the surface of Mars to the back of a wristwatch.

Thermoelectric devices are quiet, small, and astonishingly long-lived. They appear wherever one of those properties is worth more than raw efficiency. Below: eight places they actually show up.

01
Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs)
Plutonium-238 decay heat, converted to electricity by PbTe or SiGe thermocouples. Powered Voyager 1 & 2 (still running, 47 years on), Cassini, Curiosity, Perseverance, New Horizons.
Space
02
Automotive waste-heat recovery
A TEG strapped to a car exhaust pipe can recover 100–600 W of electricity that would otherwise be dumped into the air. BMW, Ford, and Toyota have all built prototypes.
Transport
03
CPU & GPU cooling
Peltier modules hold overclocked processors below ambient. Combined with vapor-chamber heatsinks in gaming laptops, server blades, and high-end workstations.
Computing
04
Industrial furnace harvesters
Steel mills, glass foundries, cement kilns — all run at 800–1500 °C and vent enormous waste heat. Half-Heusler TEGs mounted on flue stacks can supply kilowatts per square meter.
Industry
05
Portable vaccine coolers
In off-grid regions, Peltier coolers powered by small solar panels keep vaccines at 2–8 °C during transport. WHO-qualified units are now deployed across sub-Saharan Africa.
Medical
06
Body-heat-powered wearables
A thin-film TEG on the wrist, with the skin as the hot side and the air as the cold side, generates tens of microwatts — enough to run a low-power watch or a medical sensor indefinitely, no battery.
Wearable
07
Cryogenic infrared detectors
Multi-stage Peltier coolers reach −100 °C without liquid nitrogen, cooling the infrared sensors in thermal cameras, night-vision optics, and satellite imagers.
Sensing
08
Solar thermoelectric generators
Concentrated sunlight heats one side of a TEG; ambient air cools the other. Simpler and more durable than photovoltaics in dusty, hot, remote environments — though less efficient.
Energy
§ 06 — Case Study
Voyager & the RTG

A fifty-year mission, powered by decaying plutonium.

Launched in 1977, the two Voyager probes are now in interstellar space — the most distant human-made objects in existence. Their instruments still transmit data back to Earth across 24 billion kilometers of empty space. They are powered by thermoelectric generators.

The Power Source

3.7 kilograms of Pu-238.

Each Voyager carries three RTGs. Inside each is a slug of plutonium-238 dioxide, generating heat by radioactive decay at a half-life of 87.7 years. That heat — about 2,400 W at launch, 1,970 W today — passes through silicon-germanium thermocouples and is partially converted to electricity.

At launch in 1977 each spacecraft generated 470 watts of electrical power. By 2024, after 47 years of slow plutonium decay, output has fallen to about 219 watts. Instruments have been switched off, one by one, to keep the radios running.

No other power source could have done this. Solar panels are useless past Jupiter; chemical batteries die in years; fuel cells need fuel. Thermoelectricity, with no moving parts, has run for half a century in vacuum at −260 °C.

Fig. 6.1 An artist's rendering of a Voyager probe in interstellar space. The three RTG boom arms hold the thermoelectric generators far from the sensitive instruments.
Fig. 6.2 A glowing radioisotope heat source undergoing pre-launch thermal testing. The visible glow is ~1300 °C — the temperature difference that drives the SiGe thermocouples.

§ 07 — Trade-offs
An Honest Assessment

Why every building isn't already covered in TEGs.

Thermoelectricity is not a free lunch. Its strengths — silence, longevity, compactness — are real and unusual. Its weaknesses — low efficiency, high cost, material toxicity — are equally real and explain why the technology has not taken over the world.

Strengths
Where thermoelectricity wins
  • No moving parts. Lifetimes of 100,000+ hours, often limited only by the surrounding electronics.
  • Absolute silence. No compressor, no turbine, no fan (in pure TEG mode).
  • Compact and scalable. Same module works at milliwatts or kilowatts; just stack more.
  • Works at any orientation, in zero gravity, in vacuum, under high vibration.
  • Reversible. The same device is a generator and a cooler, depending on which side you push.
  • No refrigerants. No greenhouse-gas leakage, no ozone depletion.
  • Direct DC output. No inverter, no rectifier, no AC conversion losses.
Limitations
Where it loses
  • Low efficiency. Best commercial TEGs reach 8–12%; vapor-compression fridges hit 40–60% of Carnot.
  • High material cost. Tellurium, used in Bi₂Te₃, is rarer than platinum (~$70/kg, supply-limited).
  • Toxicity. Lead, tellurium, antimony — most high-performance materials are environmentally problematic.
  • Narrow temperature windows. Each material family works only in a ~300 °C band; outside it, performance collapses.
  • Brittle ceramics. Modules crack under thermal shock or mechanical stress.
  • Contact resistance. Every interconnect adds resistance; small losses compound across 254 couples.
  • Heat-sink burden. The cold side must reject heat as fast as the hot side supplies it, or the gradient collapses.
§ 08 — Future Directions
The Road Ahead

Where the next decade of research is going.

Most of the meaningful progress in thermoelectricity now happens at the nanometer scale — in crystal lattices engineered one layer at a time, in materials shaped like noodles or sponges, in theories of heat flow borrowed from quantum mechanics.

Nanostructuring

The phonon glass, the electron crystal.

The dream of thermoelectric materials science is a substance that scatters phonons (heat) like amorphous glass but conducts electrons like a perfect crystal. Nanostructuring — building materials from grains or layers only a few nanometers across — does this by introducing boundaries that impede long-wavelength phonons but let electrons pass with their wavelength intact.

The record ZT values — above 2.0 in some lab samples — have all come from nanostructured materials: superlattices, quantum dots, nanowires, skutterudites with "rattling" atoms inside their cages.

Scaling these from laboratory curiosities to industrial products is the current frontier. Some have made the leap: nanocrystalline Bi₂Te₃ is now sold commercially with a ZT roughly 30% higher than its bulk predecessor.

Fig. 8.1 Scanning-electron micrograph of a silicon nanowire array. At nanometer diameters, the wire boundary scatters phonons far more effectively than electrons.
Fig. 8.2 A superlattice of alternating thin films. Each interface reflects phonons but transmits electrons — the central trick of nanostructured thermoelectrics.
Other open questions

Polymer thermoelectrics — cheap, flexible, printable — could turn clothing into power sources. Current ZT is only ~0.2, but rising fast.

Topological insulators, where the surface conducts perfectly while the bulk is insulating, may offer unprecedented decoupling of σ and κ.

Spin-caloritronics uses the electron's spin, not just its charge, to carry heat — an entirely new thermoelectric mechanism, still mostly theoretical.
